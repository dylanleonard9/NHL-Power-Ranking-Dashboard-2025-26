Where the gap comes from

A team’s gap between actual and expected goal differential has two parts: scoring more than expected (finishing), and allowing fewer than expected (goaltending and shot-stopping). The black tick marks the total.

Methodology

Data

Two sources, both for the 2025–26 regular season, 32 teams, 82 games each. MoneyPuck supplies the shot and goal data: the “all situations” rows (even strength, power plays, penalty kills, 4-on-4, 3-on-3 and the rest) for the main ranking, plus the 5-on-4 and 4-on-5 rows for special teams. Sports-Reference supplies the standings: wins, losses, overtime losses, points, and its schedule strength figure.

Checks run before building: every team has 82 games in both files, wins plus losses plus overtime losses equal 82 for every team, points equal 2 × wins + overtime losses, and total goals for equal total goals against in each file. The standings list 8,205 goals and MoneyPuck 8,086. The difference is probably shootout goals, which standings credit and MoneyPuck leaves out, so this dashboard uses MoneyPuck’s goals everywhere except the record and points columns.

Composite score

For each factor, z = (x − mean) / sd across the 32 teams. The score is Σ wᵢ·zᵢ / Σ wᵢ. The five factors are:

  • Expected goals share: expected goals for ÷ (expected goals for + against).
  • High-danger share: the same ratio using only high-danger chances.
  • Special teams: power-play expected goals for per 60 minutes (5-on-4) minus penalty-kill expected goals against per 60 minutes (4-on-5).
  • Goal differential per game: what actually happened on the scoreboard.
  • Schedule strength: Sports-Reference’s figure for average opponent strength, where positive means a tougher schedule. It is reported to two decimals and ranges only from −0.07 to +0.06, so it is off by default and moves the rankings little.

The default weights (40 / 20 / 15 / 25 / 0) favor process over results, because chance quality tends to carry over from one stretch of games to the next better than goals do. They are a judgment call, which is why the sliders exist.

Points vs. shot quality

A least-squares line, points = a + b × expected goal differential, fitted on the 32 teams. The gap between a team’s actual points and the line is the number shown as points above or below what its shot quality predicts. Rank vs. standings compares a team’s rank on your weights with its rank by points.

Reading the “luck” charts

MoneyPuck’s expected goals model values each shot by its location and type, but it does not know who took it or who was in goal. So the gap between actual and expected is not pure luck. Elite goalies and elite shooters really do beat the model. Treat large gaps as a reason to look closer, not as proof a team will regress.

What this version can’t do

  • No week-by-week ranking history. The team data is one row per team for the whole season, so this is a full-season view.
  • No backtest, since that also needs game-level team data.
  • The schedule strength figure is coarse, so treat it as a tiebreaker rather than a driver.

Methodology

Data

MoneyPuck’s lines and pairings file for the 2025–26 regular season: forward lines (three forwards) and defense pairs (two defensemen), at 5-on-5 only. The file keeps only combinations that played at least 10 minutes together, so short-lived combinations are missing. The listed forward lines add up to roughly 70% to 83% of each team’s 5-on-5 time. The listed defense pairs add up to roughly 89% to 105%, and a figure over 100% suggests the lines file and the team file count 5-on-5 time slightly differently. Players are listed by last name only.

Measures

  • Expected goals share: expected goals for ÷ (expected goals for + against) while the unit was on the ice. I computed it directly from the two expected goal columns.
  • Per 60: expected goals for and against, scaled to 60 minutes of ice time together.
  • vs. team: the unit’s share minus the team’s overall 5-on-5 expected goals share, in percentage points. Positive means the unit does better than the team does on average.

Reading it carefully

  • Samples are small. Over 100 minutes together, a unit’s share can swing a lot from random variation, which is why the minimum-minutes slider exists. Be most careful with the extremes in the league lists.
  • Context is missing. The file has no zone starts or opponent quality, so a strong unit may simply have faced weaker opponents or started in better spots.
  • Some names look clipped. The source file drops accented letters, so a name like Stützle shows as “Sttzle”. I left the names as they come.
  • Units overlap. The same player appears in many combinations, and two players with the same last name on one team can look like one unit.
  • Expected goals don’t know the goalie or the shooter, so the goal columns can differ from what the shots suggest for the reasons covered on the Team rankings page.
